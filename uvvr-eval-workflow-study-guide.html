1. The core thesis

Most AI work is not wholly unverifiable. It is a mixture of mechanically checkable requirements, observable consequences, evidence-grounded claims, subjective qualities, and delayed outcomes.

You do not make subjectivity objectively true. You redesign the task so that more of the intended quality leaves independently checkable evidence.

The goal is therefore not a universal “subjectivity compiler.” The goal is a workflow that separates three categories:

Verified

Formal predicates, execution, hidden tests, external state, immutable records, or independently reproducible measurements.

Calibrated proxy

Evidence-grounded scores, rubrics, learned judges, comparisons, and simulations whose error has been measured.

Unverifiable remainder

Novelty, taste, long-term impact, strategy, ethics, or context-sensitive judgment that still requires humans or future outcomes.

Forbidden outcomes

Privacy violations, unsafe actions, corrupted state, unauthorized spending, fabricated evidence, or other failures that must be gated independently.

intended quality = verified portion + calibrated proxy portion + explicit remainder
Do not silently upgrade a model judgment into verification. A deterministic program generated from a subjective rubric executes repeatably, but repeatability alone does not prove that the rubric captures human intent.

2. The verification ladder

Route every success criterion to the strongest available rung. Stop as high as the criterion allows, and record what remains unmeasured.

3. The eval compiler workflow

The workflow converts a product objective into a versioned eval package and a verification ledger.

1. ObserveCollect real tasks, good outputs, failures, constraints, systems, and evidence.
2. ContractDecompose intent into success criteria, quality dimensions, and forbidden outcomes.
3. CompileRoute each criterion to V4–V0 and generate the strongest feasible grader.
4. TransformCreate known-answer, metamorphic, round-trip, differential, or simulated tasks.
5. GenerateProduce normal, boundary, negative, adversarial, counterfactual, and OOD cases.
6. CalibrateTest graders on known-good, known-bad, valid-alternative, and hacking outputs.
7. ExecuteRun isolated repeated trials; measure reliability, cost, latency, and drift.
8. LedgerReport verified coverage, proxy coverage, unresolved risk, and release gates.

This is an eval-driven workflow: define the contract before changing the AI system, then rerun the same versioned capability and regression evals after every prompt, model, tool, policy, or harness change.

4. Phase one: build the intent contract

Do not start by asking an LLM to generate test cases from a one-line product description. First collect evidence about what the system is supposed to accomplish.

Inputs to collect

  • Representative user requests and workflows.
  • Examples of excellent, acceptable, and unacceptable outputs.
  • Production incidents, support tickets, overrides, and escalations.
  • External systems, tools, databases, documents, and state transitions.
  • Cost, latency, permission, privacy, safety, and compliance constraints.
  • Evidence observable during and after task completion.
  • Outcomes that arrive later, such as user retention, expert approval, or real-world success.

Output: an outcome contract

task: produce_research_report

criteria:
  - id: citations_exist
    requirement: Every citation resolves to a real source
    criticality: gate

  - id: claims_supported
    requirement: Each factual claim is supported by its cited passage
    criticality: gate

  - id: evidence_coverage
    requirement: Major evidence and counterevidence are represented
    criticality: high

  - id: synthesis_quality
    requirement: Conclusions follow from evidence and help the user decide
    criticality: medium

forbidden_outcomes:
  - fabricated_sources
  - unsupported_high_stakes_advice
  - disclosure_of_private_data
Separate success from safety. A support agent can issue the correct refund while leaking private information. A coding agent can make tests pass by deleting the tests. Desired postconditions and forbidden transitions must be evaluated separately.

5. Phase two: route and compile each criterion

For every criterion, ask the following questions in order.

  1. Can it be a formal predicate? Use schemas, parsers, mathematical relations, solvers, proof assistants, or static checks.
  2. Can it be an observable postcondition? Inspect files, databases, API records, application state, test results, or measured outcomes.
  3. Can it be anchored? Compare against immutable evidence, a reference implementation, a known source span, or a hidden variable.
  4. Can it be decomposed? Replace “good” with atomic, falsifiable criteria.
  5. Must it remain human? Explicitly mark criteria that cannot yet be automated safely.
A

Hard gates

Permissions, safety constraints, schema validity, required evidence, budget limits, and forbidden actions. A failed gate cannot be compensated by prose quality.

B

Partial-credit checks

Coverage, progress, supported claims, correct intermediate state, and useful subgoals. These can be added after gates pass.

C

Semantic residue

Clarity, usefulness, relevance, empathy, or judgment. Use rubrics, comparisons, evidence requirements, and calibration.

D

Deferred outcomes

Long-term user success, incident rate, scientific value, or business effect. Preserve linkage so future outcomes can become labels.

Rubric compilation rules

When a criterion reaches V1, make every rubric item atomic, observable, prompt-specific, non-overlapping, and supported by positive and negative examples. Compile mechanical subcriteria into code; leave only semantic residue for the model judge.

Bad: “Is this a good medical response? Score 1–10.”
Better: separate emergency escalation, contraindications, uncertainty, unsupported diagnosis, evidence, completeness, and communication quality.

6. Phase three: transform hard-to-check tasks

Task transformation creates a checkable proxy while preserving as much of the original skill as possible. The reward can become exact even when proxy fidelity remains uncertain.

Known-answer transformation

Generate tasks from material whose answer the evaluator already knows: masked spans, synthetic corruptions, multiple choice, hidden roles, known counterexamples, or procedurally generated puzzles.

Examples: Golden Goose masks a reasoning span; ViCrit injects a known caption error; VMR reformulates open-ended material as multiple choice.

Round-trip transformation

Require a reversible journey such as document → structured record → regenerated document. Verify preservation of entities, dates, quantities, relations, constraints, and meaning-bearing structure.

Useful for extraction, translation, summarization, migration, and data transformation.

Metamorphic transformation

Alter the input in a way that implies a predictable output relation. Rename variables, reorder irrelevant facts, change a causal variable, introduce an unsupported source, or create an isomorphic reasoning problem.

Metamorphic testing detects shortcuts even when no complete answer key exists.

Differential transformation

Compare independent implementations, model families, symbolic systems, reference behavior, or expert baselines. Disagreement triggers investigation rather than automatically defining truth.

Self-play or adversarial transformation

Embed the skill in a game with known outcomes: critic versus author, test generator versus coder, attacker versus defender, negotiator under a known budget, or researcher versus evidence auditor.

Always validate transfer back to the original task; a game may reward deception or narrow strategies.

Simulation transformation

Create a small observable world: synthetic CRM, customer-support sandbox, mock operating environment, ledger, lab simulator, or digital twin. Score actual state rather than asking an LLM whether a screenshot or narrative looks successful.

Prediction or held-out-future transformation

Reward an intermediate artifact when it improves prediction of a held-out continuation or future observation. This measures predictive usefulness, but only relative to the chosen downstream model and data.

Two validations are required. First, confirm that the transformed task has a reliable label. Second, independently test whether performance transfers to the original capability.

8. Phase five: validate the verifier

A grader is another model or program under test. Static agreement on ordinary outputs is not enough; it must survive optimization pressure.

Calibration set

  • Known-good outputs.
  • Obvious and subtly bad outputs.
  • Valid alternative solutions unlike the reference.
  • Outputs satisfying the rubric literally while violating intent.
  • Verbose, persuasive, or rubric-quoting attacks.
  • Fabricated evidence and misleading citation placement.
  • Attempts to modify, skip, or neutralize checks.

Metrics for the grader

False-positive rate

How often bad work is accepted. This is the most dangerous error during optimization.

False-negative rate

How often valid alternatives are rejected, reducing exploration and diversity.

Calibration

Whether stated confidence matches actual correctness across difficulty bands.

Robustness

Stability across position, style, length, paraphrase, irrelevant content, and adversarial pressure.

Model-judge controls

  • Shuffle candidate order and hide model identity.
  • Prefer pairwise decisions for difficult relative judgments.
  • Use an evaluator from a different model family where practical.
  • Require evidence and an explicit UNVERIFIABLE option.
  • Keep an independent judge and human sample outside the development loop.
  • Track disagreement rather than averaging it away.
Why false positives dominate risk: a false negative wastes a valid rollout; a false positive teaches the policy that an invalid strategy is successful and causes the exploit to be amplified.

9. Phase six: execute in isolation

The policy must not be able to write to hidden cases, expected answers, reference evidence, grader code, reward calculations, logs, or evaluator configuration. Treat the verifier as privileged security infrastructure.

Use capability evals to test new abilities and regression evals to protect existing behavior. Version the model, prompt, tools, environment, cases, graders, and thresholds together.

Recommended release logic

       and verified_score_above_threshold
       and proxy_score_calibrated
       and independent_holdout_passes
       and unresolved_risk_is_accepted

Keep verified and proxy scores separate. A high semantic score must never compensate for a failed privacy, safety, evidence, permission, or state-integrity gate.

11. Phase eight: learn from production

  1. Preserve the failing input, trace, environment state, and outcome.
  2. Remove private or identifying information.
  3. Identify which success criterion or forbidden outcome was missed.
  4. Determine whether the policy, task, rubric, checker, or harness failed.
  5. Add the smallest reproducible regression case.
  6. Add adjacent mutations and counterfactuals.
  7. Run the case against historical and current system versions.
  8. Update the grader only after testing it against a fixed external holdout.

A dynamic verifier may need refreshed tests, rubrics, evidence, and adversarial cases as the policy improves. Preserve a stable audit set so policy and verifier cannot drift together unnoticed.

Example C: creative-writing assistant

  • V4: requested length, format, point of view, required entities, banned phrases, structural constraints.
  • V2: continuity against a story bible; round-trip extraction of characters, timeline, and facts.
  • V1: prompt-specific rubrics for voice, pacing, coherence, novelty, and emotional trajectory.
  • V0: blinded reader preference, intended emotional effect, publication or engagement outcomes.

Do not claim to verify creativity. Verify constraints and continuity, calibrate aesthetic proxies, and preserve human taste as a separate layer.

13. Minimal product architecture

A useful first version needs five components, not a platform.

1

Intent interview

Collects examples, failures, constraints, external state, evidence, and delayed outcomes.

2

Criterion compiler

Routes each requirement to V4–V0 and records gates, confidence, and blind spots.

3

Eval generator

Creates normal, boundary, adversarial, metamorphic, counterfactual, OOD, and regression families.

4

Grader validator

Measures false positives, false negatives, calibration, robustness, and exploitability.

5

Runner and ledger

Executes isolated repeated trials and reports verified coverage, proxies, cost, and unresolved risk.

A single command-line workflow and plain YAML/JSON artifacts are enough for v1. Add databases, hosted orchestration, plugins, and collaborative workflows only after real usage demonstrates the need.

14. Failure modes to design around

Specification incompleteness

The checker perfectly validates an incomplete contract. Hidden tests, mutation testing, counterexamples, forbidden-state checks, and external review reduce this gap but cannot eliminate natural-language ambiguity.

Verifier gaming

The policy finds an accepted shortcut: instance enumeration instead of rule induction, keyword stuffing, test deletion, parser exploitation, or persuasive grader-facing language.

Reward tampering

The agent modifies tests, grader code, logs, evidence, or reward state. Evaluator isolation and immutable assets are security requirements.

Rubric omission

Responses improve measured completeness while factuality, relevance, concision, or safety decline because those properties were absent or underweighted.

Process/outcome mismatch

A correct final answer may come from a lucky guess, invalid reasoning, memorization, unsafe actions, or prohibited tools. Check process invariants where they matter.

Proxy transfer failure

A model wins the synthetic game or reconstruction task without improving the original capability. Always retain original-task holdouts and human/outcome evaluation.

Correlated evaluators

Self-reward and model panels can share training errors, style preferences, and factual misconceptions. Use cross-family disagreement, references, and human calibration.

Co-evolutionary drift

Policy and verifier adapt to each other and form mutually convenient conventions. Preserve fixed external holdouts and periodic fresh audits.

The critical graph is training reward versus independent original-task quality. When they diverge, the system is learning the verifier rather than the capability.

15. Questions to answer before building

  • Who is the first user: model lab, AI product team, evaluator, or regulated enterprise?
  • What is the initial task family: research, support, coding agents, medical QA, or general chat?
  • Which external state and evidence can the workflow access?
  • What percentage of the objective can realistically reach V4–V2?
  • Which failures are release-blocking rather than score-reducing?
  • Who supplies and adjudicates calibration examples?
  • How will the system measure grader false positives under adversarial pressure?
  • What data remains hidden from the policy and developers tuning prompts?
  • How are production outcomes linked back to eval cases?
  • How will the ledger communicate uncertainty without becoming overwhelming?

A sensible first experiment

  1. Choose one narrow task with 30–50 real examples.
  2. Write the outcome contract manually.
  3. Route criteria through the ladder and build only the highest-value graders.
  4. Create 20 known-good, 20 known-bad, and 20 adversarial calibration outputs.
  5. Measure grader errors before evaluating the AI system.
  6. Run three system variants and compare the verification ledger with blind human review.
  7. Study where ledger results and human judgment disagree.
