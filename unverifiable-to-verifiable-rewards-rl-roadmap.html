Unverifiable → verifiable rewards in reinforcement learning

How the field moved from hand-written rewards and human preferences toward executable checkers, formal proofs, grounded environments, task transformations, structured rubrics, and verifier-robust RL—and why no route has eliminated the unverifiable remainder.

1. The field in one map

The field does not convert arbitrary human values into objective truth. It changes the task, output, environment, or evidence interface until some useful part of success becomes independently checkable.
Unverifiable goal“Write a good report.” “Give safe advice.” “Make a wise strategy.”
Expose structureOutcomes, constraints, claims, process steps, forbidden states, references.
Attach a checkerProof, execution, environment state, source evidence, rubric, or future outcome.
Audit the gapWhat the checker proves, misses, misgrades, or invites the policy to exploit.

The fundamental split

Route A

Learn a proxy

Use human preference, AI feedback, a reward model, an LLM judge, or a rubric to approximate an objective that remains hard to check.

Examples: RLHF, RLAIF, Constitutional AI, generative reward models, Rubrics as Rewards.

Route B

Reformulate the task

Make an output executable, require a proof, inspect environmental state, inject a known latent variable, or manufacture a proxy task with ground truth.

Examples: unit tests, Lean, SWE-bench, Golden Goose, ViCrit, VMR, SpyRL.

Route B gives stronger verification but covers fewer human objectives. Route A covers more open-ended work but retains evaluator error and reward hacking. Modern systems increasingly combine them.

2. Why rewards become unverifiable

A reward is not unverifiable merely because the output is long. It is unverifiable when the intended quality cannot be adjudicated cheaply, independently, and reliably from available evidence.

No unique ground truth

Several summaries, strategies, designs, or stories may all be excellent for different reasons.

Delayed consequences

The value of medical advice, a business decision, or scientific research may appear months or years later.

Hidden counterfactuals

We observe what happened after one decision, not what would have happened after every alternative.

Contested values

Helpfulness, fairness, creativity, importance, and ethics depend on people, culture, and context.

Partial observability

The evaluator sees the answer but not all evidence, actions, side effects, intentions, or future states.

Expensive expertise

A human may be capable of grading the work, but doing so at RL scale is slow, costly, and inconsistent.

Key distinction: “automatically computed” is not synonymous with “verifiable.” An LLM returning 8/10 is automated. A proof checker accepting a proof is verifiable relative to a formal statement. A deterministic program checking “emotional resonance” is still only a coded proxy.

3. Historical progression

1997–2000 · Specify, shape, or infer reward

Proof-carrying code established the certificate pattern: a hard producer emits an artifact that a small checker validates. Potential-based reward shaping studied how to densify reward without changing the optimal policy. Inverse reinforcement learning took the opposite route: infer reward from behavior instead of writing it directly.

2016–2018 · Reward hacking and scalable oversight

Concrete Problems in AI Safety framed reward hacking, side effects, and scalable oversight. Deep RL from Human Preferences made learned preference rewards practical. AI Safety via Debate and Scalable Agent Alignment via Reward Modeling asked how weak humans might oversee stronger agents.

2020–2022 · RLHF scales open-ended proxies

Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback and InstructGPT established the demonstration → preference → reward-model → RL pipeline. Constitutional AI replaced part of human feedback with written principles and AI critique.

2021–2023 · Verifiers and process supervision

Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems used a learned verifier to rank solutions. CodeRL, PPOCoder, and RLTF used execution and tests. Let's Verify Step by Step moved supervision from final answers to intermediate reasoning.

2024–early 2025 · RLVR becomes a named paradigm

DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO for mathematical reasoning. Tülu 3 named reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards as a post-training stage. DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated large-scale rule-based reasoning RL and accelerated the open RLVR wave.

2025 · Beyond math and code

JEPO optimized unverifiable proof data through likelihood. VR-CLI rewarded plans that improve held-out story continuation likelihood. ViCrit created a known visual-error proxy. Rubrics as Rewards brought structured criteria into open-ended RL.

2026 · Task transformation and verifier robustness

Golden Goose manufactured RLVR tasks from raw text. RLSVR/SpyRL embedded open-ended tasks in a game with known roles and outcomes. Dynamic rubrics, automatic reward specifications, noisy-verifier correction, adversarial auditing, and hack-verifiable environments increasingly treat the verifier as an evolving system and attack surface.

4. Verification-strength ladder

“Verifiable” is not binary. The important questions are what the checker sees, what it proves, its false-positive rate, its cost, and whether the policy can game or modify it.

V4Formal or executableProof assistant, compiler, hidden tests, solver, schema, exact arithmetic.
V3Grounded outcome or stateDatabase/API state, game outcome, transaction, environment transition, physical measurement.
V2Independent anchorTrusted corpus, source span, reference behavior, known latent variable, held-out future.
V1Structured semantic proxyRubric, checklist, generative verifier, LLM judge, learned reward model.
V0Preference or outcome judgmentHuman preference, user satisfaction, expert assessment, social consensus, delayed value.
Strong verification is always relative to a specification. Lean can prove the wrong formal theorem. Hidden tests can omit security behavior. A database can reach the requested state through a forbidden action. Verification strength and specification completeness are separate.

5. Eight technical routes from unverifiable to more verifiable

1

Formalize the output

Natural language → theorem, program contract, SAT/SMT formula, solver model
MechanismTranslate the requested property into a formal language with a small trusted checker.
What becomes verifiableWhether the artifact satisfies the formal statement.
ExamplesDeepSeek-Prover, Lean/Dafny RL, solver-informed optimization.
Residual gapAutoformalization may encode the wrong intent; proof quality and usefulness remain separate.
2

Make the answer executable

Plausible text/code → observable behavior under hidden tests
MechanismRun code, execute queries, compare reference behavior, or test repository state in a sandbox.
What becomes verifiableWhether observed behavior passes the test suite.
ExamplesCodeRL, SWE-RL, CURE.
Residual gapIncomplete tests, security beyond coverage, maintainability, user intent, and verifier tampering.
3

Instrument the environment

Judge the world after action, not the prose describing it
MechanismSet up a controlled world and inspect files, databases, apps, transactions, or state transitions after the rollout.
What becomes verifiableWhether the required postcondition exists and forbidden transitions occurred.
ExamplesSWE-bench, WebArena, OSWorld, τ-bench.
Residual gapUnobserved side effects, privacy, causal attribution, long-term outcomes, and simulator fidelity.
4

Manufacture known-answer proxy tasks

Raw unverifiable material → transformed task with ground truth by construction
MechanismMask known spans, inject known errors, reformulate as multiple choice, generate puzzles, or preserve a verified reference execution.
What becomes verifiableThe answer to the transformed task.
ExamplesGolden Goose, ViCrit, VMR-RLVR, Recursive Synthesis.
Residual gapProxy fidelity: success may not transfer to the original open-ended capability.
5

Embed the skill in self-play or a game

Subjective skill → objective win condition under designed rules
MechanismIntroduce hidden roles, asymmetric information, adversaries, budgets, voting, or coder–tester interaction.
What becomes verifiableWho won, which role was identified, whether the budget held, or whether tests distinguished solutions.
ExamplesAbsolute Zero, SpyRL, negotiation with RLVR.
Residual gapMechanism design determines values; games may reward manipulation, deception, or narrow strategies.
6

Decompose quality into rubrics and checklists

One vague scalar → named prompt-specific criteria
MechanismExtract atomic requirements, score each independently, and compile mechanical criteria into code where possible.
What becomes verifiableHard criteria can become V4; semantic criteria become more auditable V1 signals.
ExamplesRLCF, RaR, OpenRubrics, ARES.
Residual gapThe rubric can omit quality dimensions and the judge can misapply good criteria.
7

Anchor reward to references, evidence, or likelihood

Holistic quality → agreement with an independent artifact
MechanismCheck atomic claims against sources, compare ordered reference signals, or reward reasoning that improves held-out prediction likelihood.
What becomes verifiableLocal evidence support, reference agreement, or predictive usefulness.
ExamplesWebGPT, FActScore, RLVRR, VR-CLI, CorVer.
Residual gapCitation is not entailment; entailment is not coverage; prediction is not human value.
8

Train, combine, and harden verifiers

Imperfect checker → calibrated, adversarially tested verifier stack
MechanismUse generative verifiers, process reward models, cross-family panels, noisy-label corrections, appeals, adversarial auditors, and verifier refresh.
What becomes verifiableNothing new automatically; reliability of proxy and partial checks improves and is measured.
ExamplesGenerative Verifiers, Agentic Reward Modeling, Noisy Rewards, Adversarial Reward Auditing.
Residual gapLearned verifiers remain capability-limited, correlated, manipulable, and vulnerable to distribution shift.

7. Why “verifiable” systems still fail

Goodhart and specification gaming

Once a measure becomes a target, the policy searches for ways to increase the measure without producing the intended outcome. LLMs Gaming Verifiers shows extensional checkers rewarding instance enumeration instead of general rule induction.

Incomplete specifications and tests

A formal checker can certify the wrong theorem. Unit tests can omit important behavior. Automating Formal Verification documents specification hacking in Dafny tasks.

Rubric and judge failure are different

A judge can apply a criterion incorrectly, or the criterion can omit something essential. Reward Hacking in Rubric-Based RL empirically separates these failure sources.

False positives are amplified

A false negative wastes a valid sample. A false positive tells RL that an exploit works, causing optimization to increase it. High verifier precision is often more important than broad but permissive coverage.

Reward tampering

If an agent can edit tests, reward code, logs, or reference assets, it can attack the channel itself. Sycophancy to Subterfuge demonstrates rare generalization to reward tampering in a controlled setup.

Process–outcome mismatch

Correct answers can come from memorization, lucky guesses, invalid reasoning, or forbidden actions. Process checks help only when intermediate correctness can itself be validated.

Proxy transfer failure

Golden Goose, ViCrit, multiple choice, self-play, and simulations can have perfect labels yet teach the wrong transferable skill. Original-task holdouts remain necessary.

Co-evolution and correlated judges

Policy and verifier can drift together. Multiple models may share training errors and preferences. Preserve fixed external holdouts, cross-family evaluation, and human outcome checks.

8. Practical verifier design checklist

This extends the RLVR Book's practical checklist with the trust-boundary, false-positive, and proxy-transfer checks emphasized across the broader literature.

A. Define exactly what is being verified

Design questionWhy it mattersRequired artifact or test
What object receives the check?Answer, extracted field, reasoning step, action, final state, or full trajectory imply different claims.Name the verifier input type and granularity.
What evidence may the verifier consume?Hidden context, references, tools, and privileged state can silently change what reward means.Enumerate every input and its provenance.
What remains unobserved?The policy can optimize actions, side effects, or qualities that the checker never sees.Write an explicit “off-screen / unverifiable remainder” section.
What proposition does a pass establish?Prevents “test passed” from being inflated into “the whole task is correct.”One precise claim per verifier, including scope and exclusions.

B. Design the reward interface

Design questionFailure if ignoredRequired artifact or test
Should reward be binary, graded, or process-dense?A correct checker may still produce sparse or misleading credit assignment.Explain how reward granularity maps to the learning problem.
Are parsing, extraction, and normalization specified?Formatting, answer delimiters, or superficial syntax become the optimized task.Canonical parser plus equivalence and malformed-output tests.
Are hard gates separated from partial credit?A high soft score can compensate for safety, permission, or correctness failure.Gate equation and examples where compensation is forbidden.
How are abstention and unverifiable cases rewarded?The policy guesses because uncertainty or refusal is always punished.Known unanswerable cases and explicit abstention policy.

C. Validate the verifier before optimizing against it

Design questionFailure if ignoredRequired artifact or test
Does a known-good reference pass?The instrument rejects the intended solution.Positive self-test.
Does a plausible known-bad reference fail?The verifier checks only the happy path.Negative self-test designed around a likely shortcut.
What are false-positive and false-negative rates?RL amplifies accepted exploits; valid diversity may be suppressed.Human/expert calibration set with confidence intervals.
Has the high-reward tail been audited?Best-of-N and RL select rare exploits missed by average-case validation.Manually or independently inspect top-scoring outputs after search.
Are valid alternative solutions represented?A narrow reference checker teaches imitation rather than correctness.Equivalence classes, counterexamples, and alternative references.
Are adversarial and metamorphic tests included?Surface shortcuts survive ordinary test data.Invariant transformations, counterfactuals, parser attacks, rubric stuffing.

D. Protect the reward channel

Design questionFailure if ignoredRequired artifact or test
Can the policy read hidden tests or audit checks?The policy learns the suite rather than the skill.Separate visible feedback from hidden audit assets.
Can the policy write graders, logs, references, or reward state?Reward tampering becomes easier than completing the task.Read-only evaluator boundary and explicit permissions test.
Is generated verifier code sandboxed?Judge code can execute unsafe actions or inspect privileged data.Sandbox, resource limits, allowlisted interfaces—or exclude generated code.
Are tool calls and environment transitions logged?Harness bugs and forbidden actions appear as legitimate success.Immutable trace containing tools, inputs, outputs, and state changes.

E. Track validity during and after RL

Design questionFailure if ignoredRequired artifact or test
Does verifier accuracy drift across checkpoints?A checker calibrated on the base policy may fail on optimized outputs.Re-run the fixed calibration and adversarial sets per checkpoint.
Is the verifier and harness versioned?Behavior changes cannot be attributed to policy versus checker patches.Version IDs for task data, parser, verifier, environment, and thresholds.
Are policy gains separated from test-time search?Sampling more candidates is misreported as learned model improvement.Report policy pass@1 separately from pass@k/search system performance.
Are “tax” regressions measured?Target accuracy rises while safety, calibration, diversity, latency, or general ability falls.Frozen regression suite outside the optimized domain.
Does proxy-task gain transfer to the original task?A perfectly scored transformation teaches the wrong skill.Independent original-task holdout, ideally with human or real-outcome evaluation.
Is an external audit set kept fixed?Policy and adaptive verifier drift together while reporting progress.Never-trained-on, independently owned audit split.

Minimum go/no-go gate before RL

  • GO only if: the verified object and claim are precise; good passes; plausible bad fails; false positives are measured; hidden assets are isolated; evaluator inputs and versions are logged; and the original-task holdout exists.
  • NO-GO if: the same policy authors and freely edits its checker, only average-case examples were tested, the reward's high tail was never inspected, or an LLM judge score is being described as ground truth.
  • HYBRID / PILOT if: deterministic gates are strong but semantic residue remains. Cap the proxy contribution, require human review, and treat the result as partially verified.

9. What the evidence currently supports

Established

Executable feedback is powerful

Math, code, proof, and controlled agent environments support cheap repeated RL because success can be checked automatically.

Established

Decomposition beats one opaque score

Process supervision, checklists, and rubrics usually improve credit assignment and interpretability compared with one holistic reward.

Established

Optimization finds blind spots

Verifier robustness must be tested under optimization pressure, not only through static accuracy on ordinary outputs.

Established

Task transformations scale data

Known-answer transformations produce large quantities of RLVR material and sometimes transfer beyond the proxy domain.

Promising

Dynamic and co-evolving rubrics

They may prevent saturation as policies improve, but can drift or collude without fixed external evaluation.

Promising

Self-play for open-ended skills

Known game outcomes can train writing, rhetoric, or social reasoning; broad transfer is not yet established.

Unsolved

Universal intent verification

No system faithfully and cheaply verifies arbitrary natural-language goals, creativity, ethics, or strategy.

Unsolved

Novel value before outcomes

Genuinely new scientific or conceptual ideas can lack a verifier until future use demonstrates their importance.

10. The current research frontier

  1. Verification engineering: treat dataset, task generator, harness, checker, optimizer, and feedback interface as one adaptive system.
  2. Task transformation: discover proxy environments whose labels are exact and whose skills transfer to original open-ended tasks.
  3. Automatic specification: generate rubrics, checklists, and executable judge code while testing what cannot be compiled faithfully.
  4. Process-level verification: attach deterministic checks to intermediate states rather than broadcasting one terminal reward across an entire trajectory.
  5. Verifier robustness: model asymmetric noise, create appeal mechanisms, instrument known hacks, and train adversarial auditors.
  6. Grounded agents: turn digital work into measurable application state and eventually connect rewards to real-world outcomes.
  7. Scalable oversight: preserve an evaluator advantage through debate, prover–verifier games, tools, legibility, and decomposed evidence.
  8. Adaptive verification: refresh tasks and verifiers before the policy saturates or exploits them while maintaining a fixed external audit set.
The frontier is not “make every reward verifiable.” It is “expand the independently checkable surface, measure proxy error, isolate the reward channel, and expose the remainder that still depends on judgment or time.”

12. Working glossary

Reward

The scalar or structured signal an optimizer uses to change a policy.

Verifier

A mechanism that checks an output, state, step, or trajectory and produces feedback.

Oracle

A trusted source of the correct label. In practice, most “oracles” have scope and failure modes.

Reward model

A learned approximation of human or AI judgment, usually a proxy rather than ground truth.

ORM / PRM

Outcome reward model scores final output; process reward model scores intermediate reasoning or actions.

RLHF / RLAIF

Reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback.

RLVR

Reinforcement learning with/from verifiable rewards; optimizer can be PPO, GRPO, or another method.

Ground truth

An authoritative target label—not merely a popular answer or the output of a stronger model.

Anchor

An independent source, reference, outcome, or hidden variable against which some property is checked.

Proxy

A measurable signal expected to correlate with the intended objective.

Specification gaming

Satisfying the literal measurement while violating its intended purpose.

Verifier hacking

Exploiting checker weaknesses, access, parsing, tests, or learned-judge behavior.

Calibration

Agreement between stated evaluator confidence or score and observed correctness.

Holdout

Tasks or labels kept outside development and optimization to measure generalization.

Metamorphic test

A transformation whose output relation is known even when the exact answer is not.

Unverifiable remainder

The part of the intended objective still dependent on judgment, missing evidence, counterfactuals, or future outcomes.
